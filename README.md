# Energetic constraints reshape climate-tracking opportunities for large mammals across European protected-area networks

### Study overview

Protected areas (PAs) remain central to biodiversity conservation, but climate change is shifting temperature conditions beyond the boundaries of many protected sites. Their effectiveness therefore increasingly depends on both the time for which analogous climatic conditions remain available within a PA and the potential for species to move through surrounding landscapes.

Our study develops a climate-informed connectivity framework for 10,908 terrestrial PAs across Europe. It integrates temperature-based climate velocity, protected-area climatic residence time, current habitat suitability, physical and anthropogenic barriers, and terrain-related energetic movement costs. Functional connectivity is evaluated for ten focal large-mammal taxa, summarized into two analytical archetypes: large ungulates (the herbivore archetype) and large carnivores (the carnivore archetype).

The framework identifies where climatic persistence and movement potential coincide, where they are spatially mismatched, and where climate-tracking opportunities may be constrained by both rapid climatic turnover and low landscape permeability.

### Why this matters

Climate velocity indicates how rapidly analogous climatic conditions are displaced, but it does not show whether species can move through the surrounding landscape. Conversely, habitat continuity alone may overlook terrain-related energetic costs and discrete barriers that affect movement.

A PA may therefore retain climatic conditions for comparatively long periods while remaining difficult to reach, or it may be well connected while experiencing rapid climatic turnover. Considering climatic residence time and functional connectivity together provides a more informative basis for identifying potential refugia, movement bottlenecks, and restoration priorities.

### What this study does

What this study does

### Quantifying climatic exposure and internal buffering

1. Temperature-based climate velocity was calculated at approximately 1-km resolution from CHELSA historical conditions for 1981–2010 and projections for 2041–2070 under SSP3–7.0, averaged across three Earth system models. For each terrestrial PA, climatic residence time was calculated by dividing its effective radius—assuming circular geometry—by its mean internal climate velocity. Longer residence times indicate greater potential for internal climatic buffering, whereas shorter values indicate more rapid displacement of baseline temperature conditions.

### Developing habitat-based resistance surfaces

2. Species distribution models were used to characterize current broad-scale habitat suitability for ten focal large-mammal taxa. Higher predicted suitability was assigned lower movement resistance, while major rivers and lakes, glaciers, motorways, and border barriers received additional resistance. These models represent contemporary landscape permeability and were not projected under future climates.

### Incorporating terrain-related energetic costs

3. A common terrain-cost surface was derived from slope using enerscape and the body-mass-independent form of Pontzer’s cost-of-transport model. The resulting normalized index represents the relative energetic difficulty imposed by terrain rather than absolute energy expenditure or species-specific metabolic requirements. This surface was combined with each species’ habitat resistance and mapped barriers to produce composite resistance layers.

### Mapping functional connectivity

4. The composite resistance layers were analysed with Omniscape, an omnidirectional circuit-theory model that maps potential movement continuously across the landscape. Current density was summarized within 25-km buffers around each PA.

Species were grouped into six herbivore taxa—elk, red deer, wild boar, reindeer, chamois, and ibex—and four carnivores—brown bear, wolf, Eurasian lynx, and wolverine. Archetype maps retain the maximum species-specific current density in each pixel. Consequently, high archetype-level current indicates strong movement potential for at least one group member, not necessarily for every species in that group.

### Main findings

### 1. Climatic residence time varies strongly across Europe

Comparatively short residence times occur across extensive low-relief areas of western, eastern, and northern Europe, where weak spatial temperature gradients and projected warming produce rapid climatic displacement. Longer residence times are concentrated in major mountain systems, including the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, Cantabrian Mountains, Apennines, and Scandinavian highlands. These mountain systems may provide greater internal climatic buffering, although their refugial role remains relative and may diminish under continued warming.

### 2. Herbivore and carnivore connectivity share broad patterns but differ at key hotspots

Connectivity patterns showed substantial agreement at the continental scale (Spearman’s ρ = 0.725), but the strongest movement areas differed markedly. Herbivore connectivity was generally broader and more continuous, whereas carnivore connectivity was more spatially restricted, fragmented, and channelized.Within the upper 10% of current-density values, 67.8% of the herbivore hotspot area belonged to a single connected patch, compared with 33.1% for carnivores. Hotspot overlap between the archetypes declined from 48.0% for the upper 10% of current values to 37.5% for the upper 5%. Because both archetypes used the same normalized terrain-cost surface, these differences are more likely to reflect the distributions and habitat requirements of their constituent taxa—and the upper-envelope summarization method—than energetic constraints, diet, or trophic position alone.

### 3. Climatic persistence and connectivity frequently do not align

The most favourable combinations of long residence time and high connectivity were concentrated along mountain arcs and adjoining foothills, particularly in the Scandinavian mountains, Pyrenees–Cantabrian system, Alps, Apennines, Carpathians, and Dinaric–Balkan region. Even within these systems, however, favourable areas were patchy and more fragmented for carnivores. Much of the western and central European lowlands showed both short residence times and low connectivity. Northern and eastern regions—including parts of Finland and the Baltic states—frequently combined comparatively high connectivity with short residence times. These mismatches demonstrate that climatic persistence or connectivity alone cannot adequately characterize PA vulnerability.

### 4. Small PAs can face compound constraints

Because climatic residence time incorporates PA effective radius, smaller sites contain less internal distance across which analogous temperature conditions can shift before reaching their boundaries. Small PAs with short residence times and weak surrounding connectivity may therefore provide limited internal buffering while also restricting redistribution. However, short residence time should be interpreted as an exposure indicator, not as a predicted date of ecological failure. Whether a population persists will also depend on its climatic niche breadth and position, demography, behaviour, physiological tolerances, and access to areas that remain suitable.

### Core message

Europe’s protected-area network cannot be evaluated solely by identifying climatically persistent sites or mapping habitat continuity. Long-term climate adaptation depends on the joint distribution of climatic residence time, PA size, habitat permeability, barriers, and terrain-related movement costs. Mountain PAs can act as important climate-adaptation anchors, but they are not self-sufficient or permanent refugia. Their value depends on connected elevational gradients and accessible foothill and transitional landscapes through which populations could reach them. Conversely, apparently permeable lowlands may offer limited climatic buffering even where movement potential remains relatively high.

### How to interpret the maps

The framework is a continental screening tool for focal large terrestrial mammals, rather than a prediction of realized dispersal. Omniscape current density represents potential movement concentration and does not establish that animals currently use a pathway or that it leads towards future species-specific suitable habitat.

The analysis also uses a common slope-based energetic surface and does not represent species-specific body mass, behaviour, physiology, mortality risk, demographic responses, or fine-scale barriers. Local planning should therefore validate identified pathways and bottlenecks using future habitat projections, telemetry, genetic data, population monitoring, and finer-resolution landscape information.

### Conservation relevance

The results support prioritizing strategic improvements in movement feasibility rather than attempting to maximize connectivity everywhere. Potential actions include maintaining permeable foothill and transitional landscapes, creating stepping stones, mitigating transport and border barriers, protecting channelized carnivore bottlenecks, and coordinating restoration across national boundaries.

Different combinations require different responses: exposed and weakly connected PAs may need both internal buffering and external linkages; climatically persistent but isolated sites may require improved accessibility; and connected but climate-exposed sites require species-specific assessment of whether pathways reach suitable future conditions.

This framework can support the development of the Trans-European Nature Network, national restoration strategies, transboundary corridor planning, and broader European biodiversity policy by identifying where targeted intervention could most effectively strengthen climate-tracking opportunities.
